Orbits

Gravity is one line of arithmetic applied over and over. A ship that only ever falls toward a planet, and never stops falling, is in orbit.

When Date to be setTime 4:00 to 5:30Length 90 minutes

Section 1 · 10 minutes

Opener

Last week gravity was self.dy = self.dy + GRAVITY, always straight down. Today it points at a planet that can be anywhere. On paper: if the planet is at (400, 300) and the ship is at (400, 120), which way does gravity point, and how do you compute the distance?

Predict first. Both, before you click.

Straight down the screen, because the ship is directly above the planet, and the distance is 180.

The general rule, in case you have not met it: if two points are a apart across and b apart up, the straight-line distance between them is the square root of a squared plus b squared. That is the Pythagorean theorem, and it is the one piece of geometry this session needs. Here the across gap is 0, so the distance is just 300 minus 120.

With gaps of 100 across and 80 up you need the whole rule: the distance is the square root of 10000 plus 6400, which is about 128.

When the ship is off to one side, both differences matter and you need the full calculation.

Left: the opener. The ship is directly above the planet, so the distance is the gap in y. Right: two points that are 100 apart across and 80 apart up. The distance between them is the long side of the triangle.

Section 2 · 30 minutes

Falling sideways

orbit.py

    def distance(self):
        """Return the distance from this ship to the planet centre."""
        gap_x = PLANET_X - self.x
        gap_y = PLANET_Y - self.y
        return math.sqrt(gap_x * gap_x + gap_y * gap_y)

    def step(self):
        """Apply one time slice of gravity, then move."""
        gap_x = PLANET_X - self.x
        gap_y = PLANET_Y - self.y
        r = self.distance()
        pull = PULL / (r * r)

        self.dx = self.dx + pull * (gap_x / r) * DT
        self.dy = self.dy + pull * (gap_y / r) * DT

        self.x = self.x + self.dx * DT
        self.y = self.y + self.dy * DT

        self.trail.append((self.x, self.y))

Three things are happening. PULL / (r * r) makes gravity weaker further out. gap_x / r and gap_y / r split that pull into its across and down parts, in proportion to how much of the gap lies along each axis. And DT is how big a slice of time each frame stands for.

Frame 131 of orbit.py. The black arrow is the pull. It points along r, straight at the planet. The teal arrow is pull * (gap_x / r), the part that changes dx. The red arrow is pull * (gap_y / r), the part that changes dy. The arrows are drawn longer than the real numbers so you can see them.

pull = PULL / (r * r) at three distances. Twice as far away gives a quarter of the pull. Three times as far gives a ninth.

Round. The distance falls from 180 to about 60, climbs back to 178, and keeps cycling for all 1200 frames, which is twenty seconds at sixty frames a second. The planet's radius is 26, so a closest approach of 60 clears it.

The ship is falling the whole time. It never stops falling. It keeps missing, because it also has sideways speed, and the two together make a closed path.

The first 200 frames with dx = 3.8. At every frame the red arrow points at the planet, so the ship is always being pulled toward it. The ship is also moving sideways, along the teal arrow, so it goes past the planet and does not hit it. The red arrow is longer close to the planet because the pull is stronger there.

About 2.8 to 6.0. Below 2.8 the closest approach drops inside the planet's radius of 26, and above 6.0 the far end of the loop goes off the top of the window. At 6.5 it goes off screen and comes back, on a much longer loop. At 7.6 it does not come back: the farthest distance is still climbing when the run ends. That is the escape speed for this planet at this height, and the band from crash to escape is 2.8 to 7.6, which is wider than most people guess.

DT is a shortcut. Real gravity acts all the time, without gaps. This program applies it in jumps of 0.6. Set DT to 3.0 and the arithmetic is unchanged but the loop stops closing on itself: each pass lands a little to one side of the last, and the trail becomes a set of overlapping loops. The two rows at the bottom of the table show it. At 3.0 the ship still holds between 62 and 180, and it takes a DT of about 8 before a single jump is big enough to send it off screen.

The same ship with dx = 3.8 for 1200 frames at two values of DT. At 0.6 each loop lands almost on top of the last one. At 3.0 each loop is turned a little from the one before.

What to watch for

A ship that spirals out or flies off is almost always a bug, not DT. The step order in step updates speed first and position second, and that order holds an orbit steady even at a DT of 6. Two bugs produce the fly-off: moving the position lines above the speed lines, and the double division by r below. Check those before anything else.

Watch for students who normalise by dividing by r twice, or who forget it entirely. Forgetting it gives a pull that grows with distance and a ship that flies off immediately, which at least fails loudly.
